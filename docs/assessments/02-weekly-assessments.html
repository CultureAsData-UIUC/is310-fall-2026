Weekly Assessments 50%

Weekly assessments constitute 50% of your final grade, and are designed to keep you engaged with the readings, practicing technical skills, and connecting weekly work to your semester project.

Most weekly work is graded on a scale of 0–2 for creativity, effort, and thoughtful engagement, as well as the growth you demonstrated throughout the semester.

Weekly Rhythm

Weekly Participation (25%)

Weekly participation includes both individual engagement with course materials and collaborative work with your peers. This combination ensures you are both deepening your own understanding and learning from others when working with culture as data.

Before each class, you are expected to engage with assigned materials, ranging from reading a book chapter or article, to exploring a data visualization, to reading documentation or working through a technical lesson. These materials lay the groundwork for our class discussions, our Thursday technical work, and your semester-long project. There may be some weeks that are of more interest to you, and I encourage you to explore the additional listed materials, though these are optional.

How to read and annotate assigned materials?

Questions to consider when engaging with the weekly materials include:

  • What is the main argument of this piece? Is there an argument? What is interesting or evocative or infuriating?
  • If there is an argument, how does the author support the argument? What sorts of evidence or data did they utilize? How did they organize the structure of their piece?
  • If you did not feel there was an argument, what do you think was the author’s purpose or goal with the material, whether a project, blog post, tool, documentation, or dataset?
  • What is the likely audience for the material?
  • What connections or tensions did you identify across materials?
  • What did you not understand in the materials? What was confusing?
  • How would you connect the week’s materials to previous ones?

Overall, remember the goal is not to detail everything the author or creator says, but instead to summarize in a few lines their central points and how they relate to the other materials from that week.

One way to demonstrate your engagement with the weekly materials is to participate in our weekly seminar discussions. Your contributions could be in the form of questions about complex or confusing concepts in the readings, connections that you want to share across the assigned materials, or feedback and responses to peer contributions. More than simply talking as much as possible, though, our goal with seminar discussions is to create a thoughtful and respectful intellectual community.

Another way to demonstrate engagement is through asynchronous annotations. We will be using the Hypothesis annotation platform, with an introductory session in the first week of class. These annotations are a space for you to share your thoughts on the week’s materials and engage with your peers, and you may occasionally be asked to expand on your annotations during our discussions. You can sign up for Hypothesis at hypothes.is/signup and our course group link is available on Canvas. More instructions will be covered in class and via this website.

Participation also includes Thursday technical work. Asking questions, showing your code, explaining a bug, helping a peer troubleshoot, or contributing to group work during in-class homework time all count as meaningful participation.

For each class meeting, participation is graded on a simple 0–2 scale:

What does it mean to be prepared?

Prepared means you came ready for the day’s work.

For days with assigned readings, preparation means completing the short Hypothesis reading check before class. For each assigned reading day, add two annotations by midnight the day before class:

  1. One passage you think matters.
  2. One question, connection, or concern.

Annotations are graded for completion and basic relevance, not polish. To receive credit, annotations must demonstrate thoughtful engagement with the material. This includes summarizing key points, raising questions, and making connections with other readings or topics we have covered. Empty, generic, unrelated, or clearly disconnected annotations will not count. You may also be asked to explain your annotations in class.

Strong reading annotations are specific enough to show contact with the reading. They might:

  • Identify a key term, claim, example, or method.
  • Connect the reading to the week’s dataset, website, or example.
  • Notice an assumption, limitation, missing context, or design choice.
  • Ask a genuine question that could start discussion.

For Thursday technical days, preparation means reading or trying the assigned technical lesson before class. You may also use Hypothesis on technical lessons to flag questions, bugs, confusing steps, or places where the lesson connects to your project. Technical annotations should help us troubleshoot together; they do not need to read like formal reading responses.

You do not need to master the material before class, but you should arrive with a sense of what the lesson is about and where you are confused. A specific Hypothesis annotation, GitHub commit, code question, bug report, or in-class demo can all help make that preparation visible.

What does it mean to have contributed?

Contributed means you helped the class think, troubleshoot, or collaborate during the session. Contributions can happen in several ways:

  • Speak during seminar discussion.
  • Add a substantive question, comment, link, or example in Zoom chat.
  • Respond to a classmate’s point.
  • Help your group make sense of a reading, dataset, or activity.
  • Contribute visibly to a group lab or brief synthesis activity.
  • Ask a question during Thursday technical review.
  • Demo your code, bug, output, or workflow during class.
  • Help a peer troubleshoot during in-class homework or project work time.
  • Share a useful connection to your project or another course example.

Strong participation can look different for different students. Speaking in seminar is one valuable form of participation, but it is not the only way to contribute.

Grading Policy

For weekly seminar discussions, you must both be present in class and be an active participant in the discussion or activities to receive full credit. Simply attending class is not enough; you are expected to contribute thoughtfully to discussions and engage with the material and your peers.

For weekly annotations, you need to submit these via Hypothesis by midnight the day before class. This ensures that your contributions are available for others to review and discuss during our seminar. Your annotations should be tagged appropriately in our Hypothesis group to facilitate organization and discussion. To receive full credit, annotations must demonstrate thoughtful engagement with the material. This includes summarizing key points, raising questions, and making connections with other readings or topics we have covered.

For Thursday technical sessions, you can also earn participation credit by asking questions, demoing your code, explaining an error or obstacle, helping your peers during in-class work time, or making your technical preparation visible through a Hypothesis annotation, GitHub commit, or specific question.

Group Labs and Brief Synthesis

Groups will be assigned within the first two weeks of class after an initial survey of student interests and backgrounds, such as music, literature, social media, gaming, sports, film, or other cultural areas. This group will work together to complete active in-class activities and occasional out-of-class assignments, which will be documented and submitted via GitHub.

Some participation days include structured group labs connected to the readings and semester project. These labs are meant to help you get hands-on experience with cultural data and computational methods before applying them to your own dataset.

Your group usually has three tasks:

  1. Complete the shared hands-on activity.
  2. Apply the activity to your group’s cultural area or project theme.
  3. Submit a concise write-up in GitHub and share a brief synthesis with the class.

On group lab days, your lab work counts as the contributed part of participation. To receive full participation credit for the day, you still need both preparation and a visible contribution to the group lab, live discussion, or Zoom chat.

Structure and Expectations

  • Weekly prompts and activities: Each week, your group may be assigned a prompt or task that builds on the week’s materials. This could involve finding digital objects, exploring datasets, testing a tool, or applying a concept from the readings.
  • In-class synthesis: Some weeks, your group will briefly present your findings to the class. Not every group will present every week, but even when your group is not presenting, you are still expected to submit your work to GitHub and be prepared to discuss it if called upon. Presentations should be clear, concise, and demonstrate your group’s understanding and application of the week’s materials.
  • Documentation and submission: All group work, whether presented in class or not, must be documented and submitted to your group’s GitHub repository. This documentation should include a summary of your group’s activities, details on how the labor was divided, and any relevant reflections on the process. Clear and detailed documentation is crucial for both grading and for your group’s progress on the semester-long project.

Lab Expectations

Group labs are not a separate grade category. They are part of participation and course community. Your group should complete the activity, document specific evidence, connect the work to the readings or project, and make each member’s contribution visible in GitHub.

Brief presentations should be selective. Do not present every answer from the write-up. Share one useful finding, one problem or tension, and one question for the class.

Your participation credit for group work will be based on your active participation in the activity, the quality of your contributions, and the effectiveness of your collaboration. This includes both in-class activities and the quality of the work submitted on GitHub. Your grade will not be impacted if a member of your group is absent, though the group will be required to pivot their schedule and plan in that event.

Weekly group work must be submitted to GitHub by midnight before the class when it is due. This ensures that the work is available for review and that all group members are accountable for their contributions. Late submissions will receive half credit, as long as they are accompanied by an explanation for the delay. Repeated late submissions will result in the group meeting with the instructor.

Weekly Coding Check-Ins (25%)

Weekly coding check-ins help you practice the technical material and connect it to your project.

For most technical weeks:

  • Before Thursday, read or work through the coding lesson.
  • In class, we will review key concepts and troubleshoot together.
  • During work time, you will practice, ask questions, and apply the material to homework or your project.
  • Afterward, submit the required check-in on GitHub.

What to Submit

Each coding check-in should usually include:

  • Code, notebook, webpage, or other required file.
  • A short README.md or note explaining what you did.
  • A short ai-workflow.md note documenting how you used, tested, or chose not to use AI assistance.
  • One screenshot, output, example result, or link when relevant.
  • One question, bug, limitation, or next step.
  • Clear commits pushed to GitHub.
AI Workflow Note

Each coding check-in should include a short file named ai-workflow.md.

If you used an AI chatbot, document the parts of your workflow where AI mattered. Do not paste a full transcript. Choose 1-2 useful prompts or short excerpts that show how you asked for help, debugged, revised, or learned something.

If you did not use AI, include one sentence saying that you did not use AI for that assignment.

Use this template:

# AI Workflow

## What I Asked For Help With

Briefly describe what you used AI for.

## Useful Prompt or Exchange

Paste 1-2 prompts or short excerpts that were important to your work.

## What I Changed or Verified

Explain what you accepted, rejected, revised, tested, or checked.

## What I Still Do Not Understand

Name one remaining question, bug, or concept.

Do not include personal information, API keys, passwords, private data, or full chatbot transcripts.

Grading Policy

Coding check-ins are usually graded for completion, reasonable effort, and evidence that you adapted or reflected on the lesson. The AI workflow note is graded for completion and basic relevance. Pair programming is encouraged, but you are responsible for understanding the concepts in your own submission.

Check-ins must be submitted via GitHub by midnight before the class meeting when they are due. Late submissions will receive half credit if submitted before the final class meeting.
