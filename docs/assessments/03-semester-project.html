Semester Long Project 50%

Culture is complex, contested, and interpretive. Yet cultural life, whether it is music, art, literature, social media, gaming, fashion, food, or film, is constantly being turned into data. These transformations shape what can be searched, counted, preserved, recommended, ignored, valued, and misunderstood.

The semester project asks you to experience these transformations directly. Rather than only downloading a dataset and analyzing it, you will practice making data carefully, item by item and decision by decision, while also learning how to question datasets that already exist in the world.

You will work with culture as data in two connected ways:

  1. Create a custom cultural dataset. What is a cultural object, practice, or phenomenon that you care about, and what happens if you try to capture it in data? In this part of the assignment, you will design a dataset around cultural materials, questions, categories, and relationships that matter to you. This is creative and interpretive work: you are deciding what counts, what to preserve, what to simplify, and how to represent uncertainty.
  2. Audit or computationally extend a dataset. Not all responsible data work starts from scratch. You will also use computational methods to inspect, question, augment, compare, or contextualize data. This might mean auditing an existing dataset, tracing its provenance, using code to find patterns or gaps, or connecting computationally gathered data to your custom dataset.

The goal is not just to produce data. The goal is to understand what happens when culture becomes data: what becomes easier to see, what gets flattened, what gets lost, and what responsibilities follow from those choices. Your project should make your methods, assumptions, limits, revisions, and labor visible.

This project makes up 50% of your final grade. Each student will create, audit, document, and publish an individual cultural dataset. Each group will build the shared context around those datasets: a collective theme, GitHub documentation for reuse and preservation, a minimal-computing website about the process, and shared principles for working with your cultural data type.

The project is inspired by the Responsible Datasets in Context Project https://www.responsible-datasets-in-context.com/, which was created to help students “work with data responsibly.” Like that project, we will treat datasets as cultural objects that need context, provenance, documentation, and care. Your final work should help future users understand not only what is in the data, but how and why the data came to be.

Core Standard

Your goal is not simply to make a large dataset or a polished website. Your goal is to represent a cultural topic thoughtfully: preserve complexity where you can, explain what must be simplified, use computation meaningfully, and make your choices visible through documentation and process narrative.

Creative pivots are allowed. If your sources, methods, or dataset structure change, explain why they changed, what feedback or evidence shaped the change, and how the new direction better serves the project.

What Makes a Strong Project

A strong project does more than collect information. It shows how cultural materials become data and what is gained or lost in that process.

Your project should demonstrate:

  • Cultural complexity: Your dataset should capture meaningful features of the cultural materials, not flatten them into the easiest possible categories.
  • Interpretive choices: You should explain what you included, excluded, categorized, simplified, or left uncertain.
  • Creative data design: You should think carefully about what data you wish existed, not only what data is easiest to collect.
  • Meaningful computation: You should use computation to inspect, audit, expand, compare, clean, augment, or publish your data.
  • Validation and limits: You should check computational outputs and explain what your dataset cannot fully represent.
  • Scholarly relevance: You should use published scholarship to explain your topic, justify your choices, and show why your dataset matters.

These values apply across the whole semester. The mid-semester check-in asks you to show progress toward them. The presentation asks you to communicate them. The final submission is where the completed quality of the project is evaluated most fully.

Examples of Strong Datasets

There is no single correct kind of dataset for this project. The key is that your project should include both a custom-created dataset and an audit or computational extension. These two parts should speak to each other.

Examples of paired custom and audited datasets
Cultural Topic Custom-Created Dataset Audit or Computational Extension
Lesser-known children’s literature Create records for a small set of books, pages, illustrations, characters, themes, paratextual features, or visual styles. Decide what cultural details matter beyond title/author/date. Use OCR, image extraction, catalog metadata, or another computational method to compare your records with existing library metadata or to test what becomes visible at larger scale.
AI discourse on TikTok or other platforms Curate a sample of public posts and code themes, genres, rhetorical patterns, audio choices, visual cues, or claims about AI. Include uncertainty and context where needed. Scrape or collect platform metadata when appropriate, use text/image/audio tools to augment records, or audit what platform metrics reveal and obscure about cultural meaning.
Movie reviews and ratings Create a close-reading sample of reviews that records tone, genre expectations, references, identity markers, or claims that standard ratings do not capture. Audit an existing movie dataset from Kaggle, IMDb, Rotten Tomatoes, Letterboxd, or another source. Trace provenance, compare records, and identify missing context, transformations, or distortions.
Sports analytics and media coverage Curate social media posts about a team, player, league, rivalry, or event, recording themes, affect, memes, identity, controversy, or forms of fan expertise that box scores do not capture. Compare your custom dataset to historical newspaper coverage, sports-reference data, broadcast transcripts, or existing analytics datasets to ask what changes across media forms, time periods, and data sources.
Museum, archive, or digital collection Create custom records for selected objects that capture interpretive details, uncertain descriptions, relationships, absences, or culturally specific context. Use API or catalog data to compare your records to institutional metadata. Audit classification systems, subject headings, dates, creators, descriptions, or gaps in the collection.

Milestone 1: Proposal

Due Tuesday, September 15, 2026. Automatic extension available until Tuesday, September 22, 2026. Pass/Fail.

Your proposal defines your group focus and each member’s individual plan.

Submit a Markdown file in your group’s GitHub repository that includes:

  • Group Theme: What shared cultural area are you studying? What is included or outside the scope?
  • Individual Dataset Plans: What dataset is each group member considering? How does each dataset connect to the group theme? What cultural complexity are you trying to preserve or make visible?
  • Scholarly Context: What published scholarship helps explain your topic, sources, categories, methods, or ethical concerns?
  • Collaboration Plan: How will you communicate, organize folders, use GitHub for documentation and preservation, and prepare a website about your process?

This can be brief. The goal is to make your starting plan concrete enough for feedback.

Milestone 2: Mid-Semester Progress Check

Due Tuesday, October 20, 2026. Automatic extension available until Tuesday, October 27, 2026. 15%.

This is a progress check, not a finished project. By this point, you should have a serious draft of your dataset and documentation, plus a clear plan for how you will audit, revise, connect, or extend the dataset computationally.

Your progress does not have to take one single form. Depending on your project, you might have a custom dataset draft, an early scraped or API dataset, an audit notebook, an HTML curation page, a documentation draft, or another concrete artifact that shows the project is underway.

Component Requirement

What I Am Looking For

The mid-semester check-in is graded for serious progress, thoughtful planning, and engagement with feedback. It does not need to be polished. It should show that you are making real choices about:

  • What cultural materials you are representing.
  • Which details, categories, relationships, or uncertainties matter.
  • How computation will help you see something, check something, or make something possible.
  • How your dataset might need to change before the final submission.

Connecting Custom and Computational Work

Your custom and computational work should speak to each other. You might:

  • Use your custom dataset as a ground-truth sample for checking automated collection.
  • Merge custom-created records with API or scraped data.
  • Use Pandas or Altair to inspect missingness, categories, patterns, or outliers.
  • Use NER, LLMs, or another method to add metadata that you then validate manually.
  • Compare your own structured data to an existing dataset and explain what each one reveals or misses.

The key requirement is explanation: show how computation changes what you can see, what it risks distorting, and how you will check its output.

Milestone 3: Final Group Presentation

Tuesday, December 8, 2026. 5%.

During the final class meeting on December 8, each group will briefly present its website and collective principles. You do not need to present every individual dataset in detail.

Focus on:

  • How the website organizes the individual projects.
  • How the website tells the story of how the datasets came to be.
  • What your group learned about your shared cultural data type.
  • What principles emerged from the work.
  • What future researchers should know before reusing or extending your datasets in GitHub.
  • What GitHub history, contribution logs, or process documentation reveal about the group’s labor.

Due Friday, December 11, 2026. Optional extension available until Friday, December 18, 2026. 30%.

The final submission is where the completed quality of the project is evaluated most fully. It has individual and group components.

Making Group Work Visible

Your group must make individual contributions visible. This is part of grading, but it is also part of the course’s larger question: how can we datafy a process without pretending that the data captures everything?

Contribution Log

Your final repository should include a CONTRIBUTIONS.md file or a clearly labeled section in the main README.md.

Include:

  • Who was responsible for each major website page, section, or technical task.
  • Who contributed to the collective principles/documentation.
  • Who reviewed, edited, or tested another group member’s dataset page.
  • Which GitHub commits, issues, pull requests, or file histories show those contributions.

Individual Contribution Reflection

Each student will submit a brief individual reflection that answers:

  • What did you contribute to the collective website or documentation?
  • What peer review, troubleshooting, coordination, or editing did you do?
  • Where is your work visible in GitHub history?
  • What labor became visible through commits, issues, reviews, or contribution logs?
  • What labor was harder to capture?

The collective website and documentation receive a group grade, but individual grades for the collective component may be adjusted if the contribution log, GitHub history, peer review, or reflections show substantially uneven participation.

Grade Flexibility

This project rewards thoughtful experimentation, not pretending that your first idea was perfect. If your project changes direction, document the pivot clearly. Strong work can grow from failed sources, broken scripts, messy data, difficult categories, and revised research questions.

If you receive a low mark on the Mid-Semester Progress Check and your final work substantially demonstrates growth, you may request that the progress-check grade be reconsidered. This is not automatic. To request this, contact the instructors before the final deadline with a brief explanation of how your final work addresses the earlier problems.
