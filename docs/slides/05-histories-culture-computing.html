A Brief History: What Is a Library?

Kahle’s Ambitious Vision

“Universal access to all knowledge is possible, and I’d say it could be measured as one of the great achievements of humankind, along with putting a man on the moon or assembling the Library of Alexandria.”

— Brewster Kahle, 2007

Big Question for Today: Is this possible? Desirable? Who benefits?

But first what was the Library of Alexandria?

The dream: Collect all the world’s knowledge in one place

  • Scholars estimate: 400,000-700,000 scrolls
  • Copying texts from ships that docked in port

What Happened to Alexandria?

  • The problem: Single point of failure
  • Likely destroyed gradually (fires, neglect, political turmoil)
  • Most devastating: 48 BCE fire during Caesar’s visit
  • By 3rd century CE: collection largely gone

Legacy? Don’t have just one copy in one location!

How Did We Get From Alexandria To Today?

Before Gutenberg (~1440): - Books copied by hand (monasteries) - Expensive, rare, limited access - Estimated: 30,000 books in all of Europe After Gutenberg: - By 1500: ~20 million books printed - Prices dropped dramatically - Knowledge became more accessible - BUT: Still physical, still scarce, still expensive

Why Do Modern, American Libraries Exist?

18-19th century revolution:

  • Libraries as public good
  • Free access regardless of ability to pay
  • Democracy requires informed citizens
  • Andrew Carnegie: 2,509 libraries funded (1883-1929)

How Does Besser Define The Values & Goals of Libraries?

Beyond just “storing books,” libraries have always:

  1. Collected — actively chose what to preserve
  2. Organized — made materials findable
  3. Preserved — maintained materials over time
  4. Provided access — helped people use materials
  5. Served communities — adapted to different needs
  6. Upheld values — fought for privacy, free speech, equity

Core values emerge: - Equal access - Serving the underserved
- Privacy/confidentiality - Intellectual freedom - Community spaces

Challenges for Libraries in the 20th Century

New challenges: - Explosion of publishing (journals, books, media) - Specialization of collections - Rising costs - Copyright extensions - Competing with commercial information providers

New technologies: - Microfilm (1930s) — promises miniaturization - Computers (1960s-70s) — catalog automation - Networks (1980s-90s) — shared catalogs, interlibrary loan - Web (1990s) — everything changes?

From Card Catalogs to Digital Metadata

The Problem: Card Catalogs

Before computers: - Every library had physical cards - Filed alphabetically by author, title, subject - To share catalog info: mail physical cards - Each library maintained separately

Problem: No way to search across libraries efficiently

Enter MARC (1966)

MAchine-Readable Cataloging

Enter MARC (1966)

MAchine-Readable Cataloging

  • Developed by Library of Congress
  • Standard format for bibliographic data
  • Made catalog records machine-readable
  • Enabled sharing records between libraries
  • Foundation for online catalogs (OPACs)

Key insight: If we all describe books the same way, computers can share and search our catalogs

Metadata = Data About Data

MARC is metadata: Information that describes the content

  • Author (who created it?)
  • Title (what’s it called?)
  • Subject (what’s it about?)
  • Date (when published?)
  • Format (book? journal? recording?)
  • Location (where can I find it?)

Without metadata: Just a pile of stuff

With metadata: Organized, searchable collection

Types of Metadata (Besser’s Framework)

  1. Descriptive metadata — What is it? (MARC, Dublin Core)
  2. Discovery metadata — How do I find it? (subject headings, keywords)
  3. Administrative metadata — How do I manage it? (file formats, permissions)
  4. Structural metadata — How is it organized? (chapters, pages, sequences)
  5. Preservation metadata — How do I keep it? (format history, migrations)
  6. Rights metadata — Who can use it? (copyright, licenses)

Digital materials need ALL of these — not just descriptive

From MARC to Dublin Core

By the 1990s: Web creates new problem

From MARC to Dublin Core

  • Not everything is a “book”
  • Websites, datasets, images, videos
  • MARC too complex for web resources
  • Need something simpler, more flexible

Dublin Core (1995): 15 basic elements

Title, Creator, Subject, Description, Publisher, Date, Type, Format, Identifier, Source, Language, Relation, Coverage, Rights, Contributor

Goal: Simple enough for non-librarians, rich enough for discovery

Why Standards Matter

Without standards:

  • Every library uses different system
  • Can’t search across collections
  • Can’t share records
  • Everyone reinvents the wheel

With standards:

  • Interoperability — systems talk to each other
  • Efficiency — share catalog records
  • Better discovery — search across collections
  • Preservation — future systems can understand

What Makes Something a “Digital Library”?

Digital Collections ≠ Digital Libraries

Besser’s key argument:

“A common misperception is that a large body of online materials constitutes a ‘digital library.’ But a library is much more than an online collection of materials.”

Having digital stuff ≠ Being a digital library

What’s Missing from Most Digital Collections?

Remember library functions:

  1. Service to users
  2. Stewardship over time
  3. Sustainability
  4. Ethical traditions (privacy, access, equity)

Early digital library projects (1994-1998):

  • Computer science experiments
  • “Exercises in pure research, with few practical applications”
  • No users, no service, no long-term plan
  • Called “digital libraries” but weren’t really libraries

The Interoperability Problem

Early digital collections:

  • Each repository separate
  • Different search interfaces
  • Different file formats
  • User must learn each system
  • Can’t search across collections

What Users Actually Want

“Users want to access an interoperable information world, where a set of separate repositories looks to them like a single information portal.”

— Besser

Getting to Interoperability

Requirements:

  • Metadata standards (Dublin Core, METS, MODS)
  • Protocols (OAI-PMH for harvesting)
  • Common formats (TIFF, JPEG2000, PDF/A)
  • Persistent identifiers (DOIs, Handles)
  • Authentication systems (Shibboleth)

This is years of infrastructure work

Not visible to users, but essential for functionality

Digital Library Maturity Model

Stage Date Characteristics
Experimental 1994-1998 Computer science experiments, no users
Developing 1998-2007 Adding library functions, some standards
Mature ??? Full sustainability, interoperability, ethics

Besser (2004): We’re still not at “mature”

Question for us (2026): Are we there yet?

Library Values in Digital Form

Traditional library values:

  • Privacy (no tracking of what you read)
  • Equal access (free, regardless of ability to pay)
  • Intellectual freedom (no censorship)
  • Serving underserved (going to communities)

Digital challenges:

  • Commercial databases track everything
  • Paywalls and licensing restrict access
  • Platform censorship and filtering
  • Digital divide excludes communities

Your Annotation: Security vs. Privacy

“I can only imagine this digital highly protective libraries to be locked by many pages with verification and passwords.”

The tension: Security vs. Access

  • Traditional libraries: Design systems to protect privacy
  • Example: Circulation systems that only save aggregate statistics
  • Librarians have “risked serving jail time” to protect borrowing records

Commercial databases: Design systems to track users

Question: Can digital libraries be both secure AND private?

Who Controls Access?

Stakes: Who controls the digital library infrastructure?

  • Commercial: Profit motive, paywalls, user tracking
  • Public: Access mission, privacy, sustainability

Besser’s answer (2004): “It’s a big unknown right now”

Our question (2026): Has this been resolved? Who’s winning?

Digitization: Process vs. System

Digitization = The Process

Internet Archive book scanner, 2010

The Digitization Process

Digitization: Converting analog → digital

  1. Selection — What gets digitized? (Already political!)
  2. Physical handling — Moving, preparing materials
  3. Scanning/imaging — Resolution, color depth, format choices
  4. OCR — Optical Character Recognition (text extraction)
  5. Metadata creation — How to describe and organize
  6. Storage — Where and how to keep the files
  7. Preservation — Maintaining access over time
  8. Access systems — How users find and use materials

The Costs

From Kahle’s experience at the Internet Archive:

  • $10/book (outsourced to India)
  • $30/book (in-house in US, 10 cents/page)
  • $15/video hour
  • $10/LP record

For 1 million books: $10-30 million

US library/archive budgets: ~$12 billion/year

Technologically feasible. Financially achievable. But who decides priorities?

Hidden Costs

But there are hidden costs:

  • Labor (often invisible, often overseas)
  • Ongoing storage and migration
  • Metadata creation
  • Rights clearance
  • Preservation (technology changes every 3 years!)

Digital Library = The System

Digital library: Infrastructure + services + values

  • Infrastructure: Servers, storage, software, networks
  • Metadata: Standards, catalogs, discovery systems
  • Preservation: Redundancy, migration, format management
  • Access: Search, retrieval, authentication, delivery
  • Services: Reference, instruction, user support
  • Values: Privacy, equity, freedom, sustainability

This is the institutional work of being a library

True digital libraries need all layers

Examples: Collection vs. Library

Digital Collection:

  • Google Books scans
  • Random PDFs on a server
  • Files without metadata or preservation plan

Digital Library:

  • HathiTrust (rights management, preservation, services)
  • DPLA (aggregation, standards, open access)
  • Europeana (cultural heritage, multilingual)
  • Library of Congress Digital Collections

Difference: Sustainability, stewardship, service, values

The Scale of the Challenge

Library of Congress (2007):

  • 26-28 million bound volumes
  • ~26 terabytes of text
  • Cost to store: ~$60,000 (in 2007)

Kahle’s argument: This is technologically achievable

“Why don’t we put it all on-line? Sometimes it’s a lot less expensive, because often the selection process is more expensive than the digitization process.”

But what about the other barriers?

Three Key Questions

  1. Technical: Can we actually digitize everything?
  2. Economic: Who will pay? Who will control access?
  3. Political: What gets digitized? Whose stories are preserved?

Unlimited Hosting as Public Service

The offer:

“Unlimited storage, unlimited bandwidth, forever, for free, for those who want to share materials that, broadly defined, belong in a library.”

The model: Make it free to give things away

What they’ve collected:

  • 2,000+ bands (30,000+ concert recordings)
  • Community-contributed films
  • University lecture archives
  • Public domain books

The “Opt-Out” Approach

Kahle’s strategy:

  1. Put materials online
  2. If someone complains, take it down
  3. As long as there’s no commercial intent, communities generally accept it

Your annotation: “risky but practical”

Question: What are the ethics of this? When does it work? When doesn’t it?

What They’ve Collected

  • Books: Working with libraries, millions digitized
  • Web: 55 billion web pages (Wayback Machine since 1996)
  • Audio: 30,000+ concert recordings, radio shows
  • Video: Prelinger collection (2 million downloads/year), TV news
  • Software: ~30,000 titles

Your annotation: “As the wayback machine basically gives users access to browse older versions of various web pages, it shows that large scale archiving is already happening in practice”

Preservation Through Redundancy

“Don’t have just one copy” — lesson from Library of Alexandria

  • Copies in Egypt, Amsterdam, multiple US locations
  • “Different administrations so they’ll have different faults”
  • Technology refreshes every 3 years (“painful and lossy”)

The claim: This moment is as significant as:

  • The Library of Alexandria
