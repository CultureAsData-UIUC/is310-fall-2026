A Short History of the Word “Data”

Daniel Rosenberg argues that “data” did not begin with computers.

  • English uses of “data” appear in the seventeenth century
  • The term comes from Latin datum: something “given”
  • Data originally meant something accepted as a starting point in argument
  • Data is related to facts and evidence, but not identical to either

This matters because “data” has never simply meant neutral truth. Rosenberg argues that data has long had a rhetorical function: it names what is given so an argument can proceed. That makes Johnson’s critique especially powerful. When Neptune’s death becomes data, it is not only recorded. It is made into a premise for argument.

Data originally meant “a given” - From Latin datum, from dare, “to give.” - A datum is something granted or accepted as a starting point.

A given is not the same as a fact - A fact makes a claim about what happened or exists. - Evidence helps prove or support a claim. - Data is what gets treated as given so an argument can proceed. - This means data can be false, partial, violent, or misleading and still function as data.

The word takes off later because it was adaptable - Rosenberg argues the term becomes more familiar in the eighteenth century, but the major takeoff is nineteenth/twentieth century. - Computing and information theory gave “data” new importance. - But the older rhetorical structure remained: data still names what a system, argument, or analysis treats as input.

19th Century Tabulating Machines

Hollerith started his own business in 1896, founding the Tabulating Machine Company. In 1911, 4 corporations, including Hollerith’s firm merged to form the Computing Tabulating Recording Company (CTR) and in 1924 CTR was renamed International Business Machines (IBM). A Hollerith tabulator illustrated in a 1929 booklet is shown below.

Invented by Hermann Hollerith, the machine was developed to help process data for the 1890 US Census. It led to a class of machines, known as unit record equipment, and the data processing industry. The term ‘Super Computing’ was used by the New York World newspaper in 1931 to refer to large custom-built tabulator that IBM made for Columbia University.

Herman Hollerith’s tabulating machine was a punch card system that allowed the U.S. Census Bureau to process data more quickly and efficiently. It was used for the 1890 census and was a precursor to modern computing. The machine used punched cards to represent data, which could then be read and processed by the machine. This technology was a significant advancement in data processing and laid the groundwork for future developments in computing and data analysis. It also reflects the broader trend of datafication in the 19th century, where information about people and society was increasingly being collected, categorized, and analyzed for various purposes, including governance, research, and commerce.

Ted Codd & Relatational Databases

Edgar Codd, then working at IBM’s San Jose Research Laboratory in 1973, opened his soon-to-be revolutionary relational database model with the following declaration: “Future users of large data banks must be protected from having to know how the data is organized in the machine (the internal representation).” He was directly addressing the problems identified with the navigational paradigm: Any user had to navigate a significant amount of complexity to get at the data they were seeking. His model, articulated in “A Relational Model of Data for Large Shared Data Banks,” was a conceptual revolution: rather than conceiving of data as a simple means of organization, the database could now be used as a tool for querying data to find relations hidden within. Relational databases separated data from applications accessing that data, enabling manipulation of information through the use of a query language, whereby selection of specific data could be performed efficiently through construction of statements containing logical operators. IBM developed a prototype relational database model as early as 1974 called System R, which would later become the widely used Structured Query Language (SQL) database upon its release in 1981. However, Oracle (as “Relational Software, Inc.”) were first to commercialize the technology in 1979, at which point the relational database became the dominant form of bulk storage.

What was the problem with Cliometrics?

Cliometricians paid special interest to quantifying the lives of the enslaved but were motivated by very qualitative consider- ations — the tense and emotional tenor of the topic. In later years, Robert Fogel, coauthor with Stanley Engerman of Time on the Cross: The Eco- nomics of Negro Slavery, described the cliometric struggle with slavery as emerging from a “desire to develop a precise, emotionally detached, ideo- logically neutral analysis. When Fogel and Engerman applied statistical methods to their data, they concluded that the material lives of enslaved black women, children, and men were better, on average, than those of twentieth-century African Americans. In fact, in their estimation, slavery was an efficient and modern economic institution, quite compatible with “shifting labor requirements of capitalist society.→ An indignant Herbert Gutman published expressive critiques of their strategy. In Slavery and the Numbers Game: A Critique of “Time on the Cross,” Gutman identified the methodological problem as a “model [that] mixes two processes together: how slaves learned and what slaves learned.”15 Statistics on their own, enticing in their seeming neutrality, failed to address or unpack black life hidden behind the archetypes, caricatures, and nameless numbered registers of human property slave owners had left behind. And cliometricians failed to remove emotion from the discussion. Data without an accompanying humanistic analysis — an exploration of the world of the enslaved from their own perspective — served to further obscure the social and political realities of black diasporic life under slavery.

W.E.B. Du Bois and the 1900 Paris Exposition

For more on the visualizations, see Forrest, Jason. 2018. “W. E. B. Du Bois’ Staggering Data Visualizations Are as Powerful Today as They Were in 1900 (Part 1), Nightingale.” Nightingale, June 18. https://nightingaledvs.com/w-e-b-du-bois-staggering-data-visualizations-are-as-powerful-today-as-they-were-in-1900-part-1/.

I want to pause here because Du Bois gives us a different way to think about data, history, and visual argument.

For the 1900 Paris Exposition, Du Bois and his collaborators created a series of data visualizations about Black life in the United States, especially in Georgia. These were made only a few decades after emancipation, in a context where racist science and racist public culture were actively trying to define Black people as inferior.

So these visualizations are not neutral charts. They are arguments. They use data, design, color, and comparison to make Black social life visible on different terms. They show education, property ownership, occupations, literacy, population change, and institutional life.

This is useful for us because it complicates the question of whether data is always dehumanizing. Du Bois shows that data can be used against racist narratives, but only when the categories, context, and visual forms are doing careful interpretive work.

Afrofuturism Listserv

I also want to bring in the Afrofuturism listserv because it helps us move from data visualization to digital community and digital history.

In the 1990s, before social media as we know it, listservs were one of the main ways people built intellectual communities online. The Afrofuturism listserv brought together writers, artists, scholars, and technologists interested in Black futures, science fiction, technology, music, and speculative culture.

Alondra Nelson was one of the important figures in this space. Her work helped frame Afrofuturism not just as an aesthetic, but as a way to think critically about race, technology, history, and the future. In other words, Black digital practice was not only about recovering the past. It was also about imagining different technological futures.

This gives us another answer to Johnson’s question. If damaging archives can reproduce harm in digital systems, then Black digital practice can also create alternative infrastructures: spaces for community, memory, critique, creativity, and future-making.

Alondra Nelson under Joe Biden served as Deputy Director for Science and Society in the White House Office of Science and Technology Policy (OSTP) and was the first Black woman to lead OSTP. She has written extensively on Afrofuturism, Black tech culture, and the politics of science and technology.

Black Digital Practice and Missingness

Sadiya Hartman’s essay “Venus in Two Acts” is a powerful example of how to handle missingness in historical research. She explores the lives of enslaved women, particularly focusing on the gaps and silences in the historical record. Hartman argues that these absences are not just gaps in knowledge but are themselves meaningful, revealing the violence and erasure inherent in the history of slavery. By acknowledging and engaging with these silences, Hartman demonstrates a method of historical inquiry that respects the humanity of those who have been marginalized and whose stories have often been left untold. This approach aligns with the principles of Black digital practice, emphasizing the importance of centering marginalized voices and experiences in research and data representation.

For individual work

Your custom created dataset needs to involve the following components:

  • Should relate to your group topic
  • Should try to capture cultural complexity and interpretative subjectivity
  • Should involve some form of computation beyond a spreadsheet

What is cultural complexity?

For your semester project, cultural complexity means your dataset should capture more than the easiest categories.

It might include:

  • Ambiguity, uncertainty, or conflicting interpretations
  • Context around genre, platform, audience, circulation, or reception
  • Relationships among people, objects, places, institutions, and events
  • Evidence of absences, silences, or limits
  • Notes on categories that do not quite fit

I want to push them beyond title, date, author, and URL. Those fields can matter, but they often do not capture why the cultural object is interesting. The goal is for students to ask what they wish the dataset could preserve about the cultural phenomenon.

What is subjectivity?

A strong cultural dataset does not pretend interpretation disappears.

Instead, it documents:

  • Who made the data
  • What choices shaped the fields
  • What evidence supports each category
  • What remains uncertain
  • What another person might classify differently

This is where Johnson matters for the assignment. Students are going to make interpretive choices no matter what. The question is whether they make those choices visible and responsible.

Computation Beyond a Spreadsheet

Computation does not have to mean advanced programming.

It can mean using code or digital tools to:

  • Extract text from images or PDFs
  • Create an HTML page for curating objects
  • Check consistency across records
  • Compare categories or sources
  • Identify missing values
  • Create a simple visualization
  • Transform messy data into reusable data

This is a place to lower the intimidation level while also raising the bar beyond manually filling a spreadsheet. A spreadsheet can be part of the workflow, but they should use computation to structure, check, compare, publish, or question their data.

Examples of Computationally Creating Datasets

Computationally Create Culture As Data

What is auditing?

Auditing means investigating how an existing dataset represents culture.

You are asking:

  • Where did this data come from?
  • What categories does it use?
  • What does it make visible?
  • What does it flatten, omit, or distort?
  • How does it compare to your custom dataset?

Auditing is not just saying whether a dataset is good or bad. It is a way of reading a dataset historically and critically. I want students to treat an existing dataset as something that was made by people, institutions, platforms, or computational systems. It has a history, a structure, a point of view, and limits.

What is contextualizing?

Contextualizing means explaining what someone needs to know before they responsibly use your data.

It might include:

  • Provenance: where the data came from
  • Method: how it was created or transformed
  • Categories: what the fields mean
  • Limits: what the data cannot show
  • Scholarship: how your choices connect to existing research

This is where the Responsible Datasets in Context model really matters. A dataset by itself is not enough. Students need to explain what the data is, how it came to be, what assumptions shape it, and what someone else would need to know before using it.

Computation for Auditing

Computation can help you test, compare, and question existing data.

It can mean using code or digital tools to:

  • Check missing values or inconsistent fields
  • Compare records across two sources
  • Sample records for closer inspection
  • Trace metadata, dates, categories, or URLs
  • Visualize patterns or outliers
  • Add checked annotations or corrected fields

The point is not that computation gives them the final answer. The point is that computation helps them notice patterns, inconsistencies, and absences that would be harder to see one item at a time. Then they still need to interpret what those patterns mean.

Examples of Computationally Auditing Datasets

Computationally Audit Existing Culture As Data

How should the two datasets connect?

Your custom dataset and audit should speak to each other.

They might connect through:

  • A shared cultural question
  • A shared source, platform, community, or genre
  • A shared field like title, date, creator, place, or category
  • A comparison between close interpretation and large-scale data
  • A mismatch that reveals something important

They do not have to perfectly merge the two datasets. In fact, sometimes the most interesting finding will be that they cannot be cleanly joined. That failure can tell us something important about how culture gets represented differently across platforms, institutions, or methods.
