A Short History of the Word “Data”

Daniel Rosenberg argues that “data” did not begin with computers.

  • English uses of “data” appear in the seventeenth century
  • The term comes from Latin datum: something “given”
  • Data originally meant something accepted as a starting point in argument
  • Data is related to facts and evidence, but not identical to either

This matters because “data” has never simply meant neutral truth. Rosenberg argues that data has long had a rhetorical function: it names what is given so an argument can proceed. That makes Johnson’s critique especially powerful. When Neptune’s death becomes data, it is not only recorded. It is made into a premise for argument.

Data originally meant “a given” - From Latin datum, from dare, “to give.” - A datum is something granted or accepted as a starting point.

A given is not the same as a fact - A fact makes a claim about what happened or exists. - Evidence helps prove or support a claim. - Data is what gets treated as given so an argument can proceed. - This means data can be false, partial, violent, or misleading and still function as data.

The word takes off later because it was adaptable - Rosenberg argues the term becomes more familiar in the eighteenth century, but the major takeoff is nineteenth/twentieth century. - Computing and information theory gave “data” new importance. - But the older rhetorical structure remained: data still names what a system, argument, or analysis treats as input.

19th Century Tabulating Machines

Hollerith started his own business in 1896, founding the Tabulating Machine Company. In 1911, 4 corporations, including Hollerith’s firm merged to form the Computing Tabulating Recording Company (CTR) and in 1924 CTR was renamed International Business Machines (IBM). A Hollerith tabulator illustrated in a 1929 booklet is shown below.

Invented by Hermann Hollerith, the machine was developed to help process data for the 1890 US Census. It led to a class of machines, known as unit record equipment, and the data processing industry. The term ‘Super Computing’ was used by the New York World newspaper in 1931 to refer to large custom-built tabulator that IBM made for Columbia University.

Herman Hollerith’s tabulating machine was a punch card system that allowed the U.S. Census Bureau to process data more quickly and efficiently. It was used for the 1890 census and was a precursor to modern computing. The machine used punched cards to represent data, which could then be read and processed by the machine. This technology was a significant advancement in data processing and laid the groundwork for future developments in computing and data analysis. It also reflects the broader trend of datafication in the 19th century, where information about people and society was increasingly being collected, categorized, and analyzed for various purposes, including governance, research, and commerce.

The Census as Datafication

The census is one of the clearest examples of pre-digital datafication. It turns people into tabular data through names, households, race, gender, occupation, location, age, and other categories. Those categories are not neutral. They shift over time because the state, law, science, labor, and social movements change what gets counted and how.

In fact, in their estimation, slavery was an efficient and modern economic institution, quite compatible with “shifting labor requirements of capitalist society.→ An indignant Herbert Gutman published expressive critiques of their strategy. In Slavery and the Numbers Game: A Critique of “Time on the Cross,” Gutman identified the methodological problem as a “model [that] mixes two processes together: how slaves learned and what slaves learned.”15 Statistics on their own, enticing in their seeming neutrality, failed to address or unpack black life hidden behind the archetypes, caricatures, and nameless numbered registers of human property slave owners had left behind. And cliometricians failed to remove emotion from the discussion. Data without an accompanying humanistic analysis — an exploration of the world of the enslaved from their own perspective — served to further obscure the social and political realities of black diasporic life under slavery.

Project Connection: Cultural Complexity

For your semester project, cultural complexity means your dataset should capture more than the easiest categories.

It might include:

  • Ambiguity, uncertainty, or conflicting interpretations
  • Context around genre, platform, audience, circulation, or reception
  • Relationships among people, objects, places, institutions, and events
  • Evidence of absences, silences, or limits
  • Notes on categories that do not quite fit

I want to push them beyond title, date, author, and URL. Those fields can matter, but they often do not capture why the cultural object is interesting. The goal is for students to ask what they wish the dataset could preserve about the cultural phenomenon.

Project Connection: Subjectivity

A strong cultural dataset does not pretend interpretation disappears.

Instead, it documents:

  • Who made the data
  • What choices shaped the fields
  • What evidence supports each category
  • What remains uncertain
  • What another person might classify differently

This is where Johnson matters for the assignment. Students are going to make interpretive choices no matter what. The question is whether they make those choices visible and responsible.

Computation Beyond a Spreadsheet

Computation does not have to mean advanced programming.

It can mean using code or digital tools to:

  • Extract text from images or PDFs
  • Create an HTML page for curating objects
  • Check consistency across records
  • Compare categories or sources
  • Identify missing values
  • Create a simple visualization
  • Transform messy data into reusable data

This is a place to lower the intimidation level while also raising the bar beyond manually filling a spreadsheet. A spreadsheet can be part of the workflow, but they should use computation to structure, check, compare, publish, or question their data.

Making Transformation Visible

Your project should show at least two transformations:

  1. Cultural material becomes a working representation
  2. The representation becomes structured data

Examples:

  • A magazine page becomes a scan, then OCR text, then coded records
  • A TikTok becomes a screenshot and transcript, then thematic annotations
  • A museum object becomes catalog metadata, then a revised data record
  • A sports article becomes saved text, then fields about source, rhetoric, and event

This language comes directly from the semester project. I want them to see that the assignment is not asking for magic. It is asking them to make visible how culture changes form as it becomes data.

Before You Build a Dataset

Ask:

  • What cultural complexity am I trying to preserve?
  • What older categories or systems does my source inherit?
  • What can computation help me see or check?
  • What can computation not understand on its own?
  • What documentation would help someone reuse this responsibly?

This is the practical takeaway. If students can answer these questions, they are already moving toward a stronger proposal and a stronger mid-semester custom dataset.
